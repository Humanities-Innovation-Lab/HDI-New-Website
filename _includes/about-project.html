About the Project

The challenge. Across the research enterprise, better tools for managing and sharing data have changed how scholarship gets done—but largely not in the Humanities outside of Digital Humanities (DH) and GLAM (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums). Why the Humanities have been slow to adopt Research Data Management (RDM) and Research Data Infrastructure (RDI) is a live question in research policy, information science, and Digital Humanities, and one of little apparent interest to most Humanities researchers themselves.

Our approach. Rather than treating this as a deficit to be corrected, Resistance to Data investigates the data practices of “traditional” Humanities researchers on their own terms—through historical, ethnographic, and comparative study—and uses what it finds to identify where data policy and infrastructure could better serve them. In day-to-day work the project often uses the phrase “research materials” to bridge the gap between how Humanities researchers and information professionals describe the same things.

A note on “traditional.” We use “traditional Humanities” as a working term, not a synonym for “non-digital.” Defining it more precisely—and understanding the wide variety of data practices it contains—is itself one of the project’s research questions.
