Research Program

Across much of the Natural, Social, and Health Sciences, improved research data management and infrastructure have reshaped day-to-day practice. In the Humanities, that transformation has largely been confined to Digital Humanities (DH) and to Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums (GLAM). For the broader Humanities community, open-data policies and infrastructure are often seen as irrelevant, or even as a threat.

This is usually explained as a problem within the Humanities—a lack of training, awareness, or willingness to modernize. HDI proposes a different reading. We treat this “resistance to data” not as stubbornness or neglect but as an implicit methodological argument: a reflection of how Humanities researchers actually understand the role and nature of their research materials.

To test that idea, HDI asks questions of continuity rather than change—how researchers have traditionally understood research materials such as primary and secondary sources, artefacts, facsimiles, models, transcriptions, and compendia; what techniques and infrastructures they have developed to manage and share them; and how today’s networked infrastructure can accommodate these well-developed traditions.

HDI uses an interdisciplinary, mixed-methods design with an ethnographic framing, combining semi-structured interviews and observation with historical and comparative case studies. The research is grounded in fieldwork with active scholars and with the information professionals—archivists, librarians, curators, data curators, software engineers, and others—who build and sustain digital research collections.

This work contributes to scholarly dialogue on

  • The nature of “data” in the Humanities (and where the term fits awkwardly)
  • Humanities research practices and data cultures
  • Research Data Management and Research Data Infrastructure policy
  • The history of Humanities research and of scholarly editing
  • Digital collections, GLAM digitization, and collaborative scholarship
  • How research infrastructure can better serve Humanities work
