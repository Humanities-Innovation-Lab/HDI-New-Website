## 1. Home — Introduction  *(replaces `_includes/intro.html`)*

### Humanities Data Inquiry

Humanities Data Inquiry (HDI) studies how researchers in the “traditional” Humanities produce,
use, and share the materials that information professionals call **data** — and why the digital
tools, policies, and infrastructure built to manage research data have been so slowly adopted
across the Humanities.

Most efforts to close this gap ask how Humanities researchers should change to fit contemporary
Research Data Management (RDM) and Research Data Infrastructure (RDI). HDI turns the question
around. We start from the premise that the Humanities already have long-standing, robust — if
often intuitive and unspoken — traditions for collecting, organizing, preserving, and circulating
research materials. Our goal is to understand those traditions on their own terms, and to use that
understanding to make data management and infrastructure work better for the Humanities, rather
than the other way around.

Hosted by the University of Lethbridge Humanities Innovation Lab, HDI combines historical,
ethnographic, and comparative research to build a clearer, evidence-based picture of Humanities
data practice — past and present.

> *Grant basis:* Summary of Proposal (p. 6) — “the slow take-up of digital RDM and RDI by
> Humanities researchers”; Objectives (pp. 7–8) — examining practices “on their own terms,” the

> intuitive) traditions”; the reversal of the standard question (Figure 1 “bottom approach”). The

## 2. Research — Research Program  *(replaces `_includes/research-program.html`)*

### Research Program

Across much of the Natural, Social, and Health Sciences, improved research data management and
infrastructure have reshaped day-to-day practice. In the Humanities, that transformation has
largely been confined to Digital Humanities (DH) and to Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums
(GLAM). For the broader Humanities community, open-data policies and infrastructure are often seen
as irrelevant, or even as a threat.

This is usually explained as a problem *within* the Humanities — a lack of training, awareness, or
willingness to modernize. HDI proposes a different reading. We treat this “resistance to data” not
as stubbornness or neglect but as an implicit **methodological argument**: a reflection of how
Humanities researchers actually understand the role and nature of their research materials.

To test that idea, HDI asks questions of continuity rather than change:

- How have Humanities researchers traditionally understood research materials — primary and
  secondary sources, artefacts, facsimiles, models, transcriptions, excerpts, compendia?
- What techniques, protocols, and infrastructures have they developed to manage, store, and share
  them?
- What purpose do these materials serve in scholarly practice?
- How can contemporary networked infrastructure accommodate these well-developed traditions?

#### How we work

HDI uses an interdisciplinary, mixed-methods design with an ethnographic framing, combining
semi-structured interviews and observation with historical and comparative case studies. The
research is grounded in fieldwork with active scholars and with the information professionals —
archivists, librarians, curators, data curators, software engineers, and others — who build and
sustain digital research collections.

**This work contributes to scholarly dialogue on**

- The nature of “data” in the Humanities (and where the term fits awkwardly)
- Humanities research practices and data cultures
- Research Data Management and Research Data Infrastructure policy
- The history of Humanities research and of scholarly editing
- Digital collections, GLAM digitization, and collaborative scholarship
- How research infrastructure can better serve Humanities work

> *Grant basis:* Context (pp. 8–10) — the RDM/RDI gap, the three existing “positions,” and the
> framing of “resistance to data” as an unexpressed fourth argument (Methodology, p. 10);
> Methodology (pp. 10–12) — the continuity questions are paraphrased almost directly from p. 11;
> the mixed-methods/ethnographic design and the list of infrastructure professionals are from
> pp. 11–12.

## 3. Research — Program Areas  *(replaces `_includes/program-area.html`)*

### Research Streams

The current SSHRC Insight phase of HDI (2025–2029) runs three connected lines of inquiry. Together
they build a picture of Humanities data practice that is historical as well as contemporary,
descriptive as well as comparative.

**Historical & Theoretical — Representational data and the long history of Humanities “data”**

This stream traces how Humanities researchers have historically collected, managed, and
disseminated what RDM/RDI specialists would call data — beginning with “representational data”:
the transcriptions, facsimiles, editions, models, recordings, and photographs that stand in for
cultural objects and activities. Viewed this way, a critical edition is itself a form of data
infrastructure — it gathers representations of cultural objects, attaches detailed metadata, and
presents them for further analysis. This stream asks how those long-standing practices compare to
the assumptions built into today’s data infrastructure.

**Ethnographic — How Humanists actually work**

Through an intensive interview program with active scholars (target n≈150) across fields such as
literary and cultural studies, art history, archaeology, classics, and history, this stream
documents how researchers form their questions, work with sources and digital tools, and think
about research materials *as* research materials. Sampling deliberately balances scholars who work
with non-digital materials and those who work digitally, in order to capture both the variety and
the common ground across the Humanities.

**Comparative — Designing digital collections**

This stream extends HDI’s comparative case analysis to the design of digital thematic research
collections — sites of intensive collaboration between researchers and infrastructure
professionals. Using a “user-ecology” perspective, it examines how collections are designed to
serve many kinds of users and how that collaboration shapes new modes of computational research and
digital publishing in the Humanities.

> *Grant basis:* Methodology (pp. 11–12), three lines of inquiry described in order — historical /
> representational data and critical-editions-as-infrastructure (p. 11); intensive interviewing,
> n=150, the named disciplines, and the digital/non-digital sampling balance (pp. 11–12);
> comparative case analysis of digital collection design and the “user ecology” framing (p. 12).
> Dates from the Timeline (p. 12) and form page 1.
>
> **Note:** This replaces the live site’s three phases (2021–2022 “FAIR Humanities Data,”
> 2022–2027 “Research Practices,” 2027–2029 “Governance/Stewardship/Policy”). Those phases blended
> the umbrella program’s history with framing (governance, stewardship, policy as primary aims)

## 4. Research — Outputs & Collaborations  *(replaces `_includes/out-collab.html`)*

### Outputs & Collaborations

HDI is led from the Humanities Innovation Lab at the University of Lethbridge and carried out
through collaboration with Humanities scholars and with the information professionals and cultural
institutions who build research infrastructure. Fieldwork and case studies draw on universities and
GLAM organizations (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums).

**Project outputs include**

1. Research articles and comparative case studies
2. Historical studies of Humanities data practice and scholarly editing
3. Ethnographic accounts of contemporary Humanities research practice
4. Conference presentations and disciplinary dissemination
5. Foundations for a planned monograph synthesizing the project’s findings
6. Recommendations to help RDM/RDI better serve Humanities researchers

## 5. About — Project Summary block  *(new optional `_includes` section, or expand the About page)*

### About the Project

**The challenge.** Across the research enterprise, better tools for managing and sharing data have
changed how scholarship gets done — but largely not in the Humanities outside of DH and GLAM. Why
the Humanities have been slow to adopt RDM and RDI is a live question in research policy, information
science, and Digital Humanities, and one of little apparent interest to most Humanities researchers
themselves.

**Our approach.** Rather than treating this as a deficit to be corrected, *Resistance to Data*
investigates the data practices of “traditional” Humanities researchers on their own terms — through
historical, ethnographic, and comparative study — and uses what it finds to identify where data
policy and infrastructure could better serve them. In day-to-day work the project often uses the
phrase **“research materials”** to bridge the gap between how Humanities researchers and information
professionals describe the same things.

**A note on “traditional.”** We use “traditional Humanities” as a working term, not a synonym for
“non-digital.” Defining it more precisely — and understanding the wide variety of data practices it
contains — is itself one of the project’s research questions.

> *Grant basis:* Summary “The Challenge” / “Contribution” (p. 6); the “research materials” bridging
> term and the footnoted caution about “data” (p. 7, nn. 1–2); the explicit framing of
> “traditional” as a variable to be defined (pp. 5, 7).

## Grant Fidelity Review

A point-by-point check of this draft against the proposal, and items to resolve before publishing.

**A. Central thesis now present (was missing).** The live site never states the project’s core
intellectual move — reframing the question from “how must the Humanities change to fit RDM/RDI?” to
“how can RDM/RDI be adapted to existing Humanities practice?”, and treating “resistance to data” as
an implicit methodological argument. This draft makes that the spine of the Home and Research copy,
matching the Summary, Objectives, and Methodology.

**B. Vocabulary re-anchored.** Grant-central terms absent from the live site — “traditional”
Humanities, RDM/RDI, GLAM, representational data, research materials, user ecology, critical
editions as data infrastructure — are now introduced and (where needed) glossed in plain language.

**C. Scope tightened.** Live copy leans on “preservation, governance, ethics, and long-term
stewardship” as headline themes. In the grant these are context, not the object of study. The draft
keeps the project’s real object — data *practice* and the RDM/RDI uptake gap — in the foreground.
*Decision point:* if the public site is meant to represent the **whole HDI umbrella** (multiple
grants over 2017–2029) rather than only the current Insight project, some broader language is
legitimate. Recommendation: keep the umbrella framing for the program’s history, but describe the
*current research* using the grant’s own scope, as drafted.

**D. Program-area dates and themes changed.** The live “phases” (2021–2022 / 2022–2027 / 2027–2029,
with governance/policy as the final aim) don’t match the grant. The grant’s Insight phase is
2025–2029 with three *concurrent research streams* (Historical/Theoretical, Ethnographic,
Comparative), not sequential thematic phases. The draft’s Section 3 reflects the grant; confirm
whether you also want to preserve a separate “program history” timeline for the earlier PDGs.

**E. Funding figure to verify.** Live site lists **CA$300,000** for grant `435-2025-0705`. The
application *requested* **$400,000** ($100k × 4 yrs); a `$300,000` figure also appears in the budget
pages (p. ~40 of the PDF), consistent with an awarded amount below the request. Confirm the official
awarded total before publishing. *(No change made.)*

**F. Personnel — accurate but broader than the grant’s named team.** The proposal names O’Donnell
(PI) and Bordalejo (co-applicant) as the intellectual leads; the live Members list adds Nathan Woods
(Lead Researcher) and several research assistants. This is normal and fine — the grant lists
“intellectual direction” only, not assistants/students. No change needed; just don’t describe RAs as
co-applicants.

**G. Numbers used.** Interview target is given as “n≈150,” matching the proposal’s expected sample
(p. 12) and the initial scan’s n=150 stakeholder interviews (p. 8). If the public site prefers not
to commit to a figure, “roughly 150” or “a large interview program” are safe alternatives.

**H. Claims deliberately *not* made.** The draft avoids asserting completed findings, specific
publications, or partner institution names, since the proposal states first articles are still “in
preparation” (p. 8, p. 12). Add those to the Outputs page only as they become public.
